\section*{Part (a) --- Gradient-descent variants}

Both plots put loss against \emph{wall-clock training time} rather than epochs,
because one epoch means something different for each method: full-batch takes a
single update per epoch while SGD takes $n$, so equal epoch counts are not equal
work. Loss evaluation is excluded from the clock, since it is reporting rather
than training.

\textbf{Which method reduces the loss fastest.} The three batched methods all
finish in well under a second and their totals differ by less than run-to-run
timing noise, so the informative comparison is the \emph{shape} of the curves,
not the totals. Per unit of time mini-batch GD and AdaGrad are clearly ahead:
both are below $0.60$ validation loss within roughly $10$\,ms, a level
full-batch GD does not reach until about $0.2$\,s and SGD not until about
$0.5$\,s. The reason is updates per unit of arithmetic --- one pass over the data
buys full-batch a single step but buys mini-batch $n/32 \approx 135$, so
full-batch does the same work to move the parameters far less. AdaGrad and
mini-batch are nearly indistinguishable early, but AdaGrad's per-coordinate
scaling lets it settle at the lowest final loss of the four ($0.4626$ train,
$0.5286$ val); its advantage appears late, once the raw gradient has shrunk and a
fixed step size becomes too small. SGD is the one unambiguous outlier in
wall-clock terms: at batch size $1$ it performs $n$ Python-level iterations per
epoch, so per-update overhead dominates and $30$ epochs cost $2.22$\,s, several
times any other method despite being the fewest epochs.

\textbf{When each model starts to overfit.} Overfitting shows as validation loss
turning upward while training loss keeps falling. Mini-batch GD reaches its
minimum validation loss of $0.5281$ at epoch $72$ (of $200$) and drifts up
afterwards, so it overfits for roughly the last two thirds of its schedule.
AdaGrad bottoms out at $0.5265$ at epoch $193$ (of $200$), i.e.\ it is essentially
still improving when the prescribed schedule ends. Full-batch GD and SGD never
turn: their minima coincide with their final epochs, so within the prescribed
budgets they are under-trained rather than over-trained --- consistent with the
time plot, where both are still descending when they stop.

\section*{Part (b) --- Class imbalance: which metrics reveal the benefit}

Training counts are $N{=}2723$, AF${=}366$, Other${=}1221$, so AF is outnumbered
roughly $7{:}1$. All four models use the fixed mini-batch AdaGrad protocol, so
any difference is attributable to the reweighting alone. Metrics on

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Which metrics actually reflect the benefit.} Two of the four columns are
actively misleading here. \emph{Plain accuracy gets worse} under every treatment
($0.7998 \to 0.7738$ for full-strength weighting), because accuracy is dominated
by the majority class and reweighting deliberately trades Normal recall
($0.9366 \to 0.8305$) for minority recall --- so a reader tracking accuracy would
conclude the treatments failed. \emph{Macro-AP is essentially flat}
($0.7765 \to 0.7748 / 0.7777 / 0.7659$, i.e.\ within $\pm 0.011$), and for a
principled reason: AP is computed from the \emph{ranking} of predicted
probabilities, while class weighting mostly rescales the logits and shifts the
decision boundary. Reweighting changes which side of the boundary examples fall
on, not their relative order, so a threshold-free ranking metric is close to
blind to it.

What does reveal the benefit is \textbf{balanced accuracy} and
\textbf{per-class AF recall}, both of which weight the minority class equally
with the majority: balanced accuracy rises $0.7186 \to 0.7555$ and AF recall
rises $0.6923 \to 0.7949$ under full-strength weighting, a $+10.3$ point gain on
the class the task cares about. The ordering across methods is consistent:
full-strength weighting moves the most, focal loss is intermediate
($0.7564$ AF recall), and \texttt{classweight2} with $\alpha^{0.3}$ moves least
($0.7308$) --- exactly as expected, since $p{=}0.3$ compresses the weights
towards $1$ and so applies a weaker correction. The cost is AF precision, which
falls $0.8060 \to 0.6200$: the treatments buy recall by accepting more false AF
calls, which is the right trade only if false negatives are costlier than false
positives --- the opposite of the regime in Part (c).

\section*{Part (c) --- Feature engineering for AF detection}

\textbf{Why the pipeline looks the way it does.} The metric is
$M = \mathrm{TP}/P - \mathrm{FP}/(N\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon = 1/100$, i.e.
$M = \mathrm{TPR} - 100\,\mathrm{FPR}$. One false positive costs as much as
$100/N$ of recall, so on a validation set with $N \approx 1000$ non-AF windows a
single false positive burns $0.1$ of $M$ while one extra true positive buys about
$0.01$. Every choice below follows from that asymmetry.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Missing values are median-imputed, then every feature is
\emph{winsorised} to the training set's $[0.1\%, 99.9\%]$ quantiles before
standardisation. Winsorising matters specifically because of the metric: a single
wild outlier at test time would otherwise produce a huge logit and a confident
false positive. All statistics --- medians, clip limits, means, standard
deviations --- are fit on \texttt{train.csv} only and applied unchanged elsewhere.

\textbf{Features used.} The submitted model uses the \textbf{392 supplied
columns} (78 baseline + 314 Goodfellow), with no raw-signal-engineered features,
so $\phi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{392}$ and $d = 392 < 500$. This was a measured
choice, not a default. We also built a second feature map that returns to
\texttt{raw\_signals/} and adds $\sim$70 columns: Pan--Tompkins QRS detection
feeding COSEn (Lake \& Moorman), symbolic-dynamics Shannon entropy (Zhou et
al.) and Lorenz-plot $\delta$RR occupancy (Sarkar et al.); atrial-activity
measures obtained by zero-padding QRST cancellation, giving P-wave beat-to-beat
reproducibility and $4$--$9$\,Hz fibrillatory-wave band power in the TQ
intervals; and signal-quality descriptors intended to suppress the false
positives that noisy strips cause when spurious QRS detections mimic an
irregular rhythm. On this dataset that pipeline did not beat the supplied
columns by enough to justify its cost, which is itself the finding: the
$392$ supplied features already capture the RR-irregularity and morphology
information that separates AF here.

\textbf{Data cleaning.} Following the course announcement that patients $45$ and
$51$ are unusually hard, both were dropped --- $1413$ rows from
\texttt{train.csv} and $1371$ from \texttt{val.csv}. They are dropped from
training and threshold selection only, never from the scored set, since every
test row must still receive a prediction.

\textbf{(ii) Class imbalance.} Class weighting was treated as a hyperparameter
rather than assumed: $\{$none, \texttt{balanced}$\}$ was searched jointly with
the regularisation strength by patient-grouped cross-validation scored directly
on $M$. The search selected \textbf{no class weighting} with $C = 0.01$. That is
the opposite of the usual advice, and it follows from the metric: `balanced'
weighting inflates the minority class to raise recall, but here a false positive
costs $100\times$ what a true positive earns, so buying recall at the price of
specificity is exactly the wrong trade --- the same trade that \emph{helped} in

\textbf{(iii) Threshold selection.} The threshold is chosen on pooled
patient-grouped out-of-fold predictions on \texttt{train.csv} together with the
final model's predictions on \texttt{val.csv}, which the model never trains on.
Rather than the raw arg-max of $M$, we take the \emph{largest} threshold whose
$M$ is within $0.01$ of the best: the arg-max sits exactly where a couple of
negatives happen to fall below the cut, and a slightly stricter threshold costs
almost no recall while protecting against the private test set placing one more
negative just above it. As an anchor, for calibrated probabilities the threshold
maximising expected $M$ is $\alpha P/(N + \alpha P)$ with $\alpha = 1/\epsilon$,
since admitting a window with $P(\mathrm{AF})=p$ changes $M$ by
$p/P - \alpha(1-p)/N$.

On the pooled selection set the sweep chose $\mathbf{0.8887}$, against a
calibrated-optimal anchor of $0.9633$. The gap is informative rather than
alarming: it says the fitted probabilities are somewhat under-confident, so the
empirical sweep --- which assumes nothing about calibration --- settles lower
than the theoretical value would. At that threshold the pooled operating point
is $\mathrm{TP}=9622$, $\mathrm{FP}=56$, $\mathrm{TPR}=0.7380$,
$\mathrm{FPR}=0.0011$, giving $M = 0.6351$; patient-grouped cross-validated $M$
on training data alone is $0.6276$, so the selection is not obviously
over-fitted to the validation split. Note where the remaining headroom is: the
$56$ false positives cost $0.11$ of $M$, while all the recall earned only
$0.738$ --- on this metric, suppressing false positives is worth far more than
chasing the last few true ones.
